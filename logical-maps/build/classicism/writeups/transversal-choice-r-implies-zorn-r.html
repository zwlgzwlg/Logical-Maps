Transversal Choice implies Zorn's Lemma

The proof follows the route through the Bourbaki–Witt fixed-point lemma in Serge Lang, Algebra, Appendix 2. It applies the Bourbaki–Witt lemma (Background, “Relations, orders and fixed points”) to the R-chains, preordered by inclusion. The step function adds a chosen point to any chain that can be extended. A fixed point is then a chain that nothing extends, and an upper bound of such a chain is maximal. The translation into C is by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), prompted by Cian Dorr, 27 October 2026.

Claim. For each type \sigma, including e, Transversal Choice implies Zorn’s Lemma at \sigma. The proof uses two consequences of Transversal Choice:

  • Transversal at \sigma, which is Transversal Choice at \sigma t for coextension;
  • Relational Choice at (\sigma t,\sigma), by the recorded result that Transversal Choice implies Relational Choice.

Preorder, Total and TotalOrder are as in Background. Everything is unboxed, and the whole argument takes place at the evaluation point.

Setting

Fix a preorder R^{\sigma\sigma t}, write x\preccurlyeq y for (Rx)y, and suppose every R-chain has an upper bound. Here an R-chain is a property C with \operatorname{TotalOrder}(R,C).

For C^{\sigma t} and y^\sigma put C+y:=\lambda z\, .\,Cz\lor z=y. Say that y extends C if \neg Cy and C+y is an R-chain. Whether y extends C depends only on the extension of C.

The step relation

The relation

(UC)y:=(\neg\exists y'\, .\,y'\text{ extends }C)\lor y\text{ extends }C

is serial, so Relational Choice gives a functional S\subseteq U. Transversal gives \mathcal T^{(\sigma t)t}, with exactly one instance coextensive with each property. Put

\begin{aligned} (FC)D:=\exists C'\,k\, .\,{}&\mathcal TC'\land C'\equiv C\land(SC')k\\ &\land\bigl((k\text{ extends }C\land D\equiv C+k)\lor (\neg\,k\text{ extends }C\land D\equiv C)\bigr). \end{aligned}

In words: to step from C, take the \mathcal T-representative C' of C’s coextension class, take the point k that S chooses for C', and add k to C if it extends C.

Applying Bourbaki–Witt

Apply the lemma at type \sigma t, with:

  • X:=\lambda C\, .\,\operatorname{TotalOrder}(R,C), the R-chains;
  • \preccurlyeq\ :=\ \subseteq, so that \approx is \equiv;
  • F as above.

The hypotheses hold as follows.

Hypothesis 1. Inclusion is reflexive and transitive.

Hypothesis 2: chains have suprema. Let Z be a family of R-chains that \subseteq totally preorders, and put J:=\lambda x\, .\,\exists C\, .\,ZC\land Cx.

  • J is an R-chain. Each of Trans, Antisymm and Total concerns at most three instances of J. Each of these lies in a member of Z, and the members are comparable under inclusion, so the largest of those members contains all of them. That member is an R-chain.
  • Every member of Z is included in J, and J is included in every property that includes all the members.
  • So J is a supremum of Z. When Z is empty, J is \bot_{\sigma t}.

Hypothesis 3: F is progressive and invariant. Let C be an R-chain.

  • Existence. \mathcal T has an instance C'\equiv C, and S gives k with (SC')k, so C has an F-value.
  • Progress. Every F-value D includes C. It is an R-chain, since it is coextensive with C+k for a k that extends C, or else with C, and TotalOrder depends only on extensions.
  • Invariance. Let C\equiv C_1, (FC)D and (FC_1)D_1. The two witnesses for C' are \mathcal T-instances coextensive with C, so they are identical by the uniqueness clause of Transversal. Functionality of S then gives the same k. Since k extends C iff it extends C_1, D\equiv D_1.

Invariance is the only place where Transversal is used. Everything else would work with S applied to C directly.

A maximal chain, and a maximal element

By part (c) of the lemma, there is an R-chain m such that D\equiv m whenever (Fm)D.

Nothing extends m. Suppose something did. Let C' be the \mathcal T-instance coextensive with m, and k the point with (SC')k. Something extends C', so (UC')k gives that k extends C', and hence m. Then m+k is an F-value of m. It contains k, while m does not, so m+k\not\equiv m, contradicting the choice of m.

By hypothesis, m has an upper bound u. Suppose u\preccurlyeq y but y\not\preccurlyeq u. Then \neg my, since every instance of m is \preccurlyeq u. Also m+y is an R-chain:

  • transitivity holds since R is a preorder;
  • every instance x of m has x\preccurlyeq u\preccurlyeq y;
  • y\preccurlyeq x would give y\preccurlyeq u, so antisymmetry holds at y.

So y extends m, which is impossible. Hence u\preccurlyeq y implies y\preccurlyeq u, and u is maximal. \square

Remarks

Two kinds of chain. The lemma’s chains are families of R-chains, totally preordered by inclusion, and they need not be antisymmetric. Zorn’s Lemma concerns R-chains, which must be antisymmetric. The two notions meet only in hypothesis 2, where a union of R-chains is shown to be an R-chain.

Relational Choice alone. If the lemma did not require invariance, the argument would run with S applied to C in place of the \mathcal T-mediated F. It would then give Zorn’s Lemma from Relational Choice alone. Zorn’s Lemma implies Well-Ordering, which implies Transversal Choice, by the recorded results. So a proof of Zorn’s Lemma from Relational Choice alone would settle the open question whether Relational Choice implies Transversal Choice.

Weak Rigid Comprehension can do the job of the transversal: choose from the weakly rigid property coextensive with C, which is unique by the recorded result that Relational Choice and Weak Rigid Comprehension imply Transversal Choice.

Earlier proof. An earlier version of this write-up followed Jonathan Lewin’s proof by conforming subsets (“A Simple Proof of Zorn’s Lemma”, American Mathematical Monthly 98, 1991). The Lean certificate formalizes that earlier argument.
