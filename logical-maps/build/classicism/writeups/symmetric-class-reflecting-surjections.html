Symmetric ideally-full model: class-reflecting surjections of infinitely many infinite classes

Cian Dorr proposed this model on 16 October 2026. He wanted a model of C in which each individual is contingently distinct from some other, but no functional relation takes each individual to one contingently distinct from it. Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) fixed the arrows and wrote this verification the same day. No Lean verification is claimed.

The model

It is a symmetric ideally-full intensional action model in the sense of Dorr’s draft Boolean Completeness does not imply Rigid Comprehension (Definitions 15–18, p. 8). By its Proposition 20 it is a model of C; for one object, that needs only a monoid of maps on the base and a group of permutations inside it.

  • Base. One object, \mathbb N, with an equivalence relation E that has infinitely many classes, each infinite. Write [x] for the class of x.
  • Arrows. The surjections h that reflect E: hx\mathrel E hy only if x\mathrel E y. So an arrow identifies two points only if they lie in one class.
    • These are closed under composition: if kh\,x\mathrel E kh\,y then hx\mathrel E hy, so x\mathrel E y.
    • Merely “collapsing only within a class” would not be closed under composition. One map could send two classes into one class, and a second could then collapse across them.
  • Symmetries. \operatorname{Aut}(E), the permutations mapping classes onto classes. They are arrows, and they form a group.
  • Domains. Each relational domain holds the intensions that are symmetric and pinned down by a finite set. The individuals are the base, acted on identically.
  • Evaluation point. The identity arrow.

Two facts are used throughout:

  • Extension. A finite partial isomorphism of (\mathbb N,E) extends to a member of \operatorname{Aut}(E), because there are infinitely many classes and each is infinite.
  • Contingent distinctness. x and y are contingently distinct iff they are distinct and x\mathrel E y.
    • If x\mathrel E y and x\ne y, a surjection of [x] onto itself that identifies them, extended by the identity on the other classes, is an arrow.
    • Arrows identify only points in one class, so points in different classes stay distinct at every world.

Every truth is ND-necessary

For finite N, let \delta_N be the set of arrows that are injective on N and preserve the pattern of E on N: n\mathrel E n'\leftrightarrow hn\mathrel E hn' for n,n'\in N. Reflection holds for every arrow, so only preservation is a restriction.

  • \delta_N is a true proposition. It is symmetric, pinned down by N, and contains the identity.
  • \Diamond\delta_N=\delta_N. Suppose kh\in\delta_N. Then h is injective on N. If n\mathrel E n', then kh\,n\mathrel E kh\,n', so hn\mathrel E hn', because k reflects E. So h\in\delta_N, and the other inclusion takes k=1.
  • Every truth pinned down by N contains \delta_N. Let p be such a truth and h\in\delta_N. Then h|_N is a finite partial isomorphism of E, so it extends to some \sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(E). By symmetry \sigma=\sigma\circ 1\in p, and h agrees with \sigma on N, so h\in p.

So \Box(\Diamond\delta_N\to p) holds, with \delta_N true, and p is ND-necessary: Truths are ND-necessary holds.

In symmetric-infinite-classes the arrows are all surjections, and this fails. There, a surjection injective on \{0,1\} can separate the classes of the images of 0\mathrel E 1, and nothing undoes that.

Modalized Relational Choice fails

Let

U:=\lambda xy\, .\,(x\ne y\land\Diamond x=y)\lor\forall z\, .\,(z\ne x\to\Box z\ne x).

U is necessarily serial by a theorem of C. If some y is contingently distinct from x, then Uxy. Otherwise Uxx holds through the second disjunct.

In this model the second disjunct is false of every individual, at every arrow, because every class is infinite. Now suppose S\le U were necessarily functional. At the identity S is then a function f with f(x)\ne x and f(x)\mathrel E x.

  1. Let N be a finite set pinning S down, and let g\in\operatorname{Aut}(E) fix N pointwise. From \langle x,f(x),1\rangle\in S, symmetry gives \langle gx,g f(x),g\rangle\in S. Since g agrees with 1 on N, pinning gives \langle gx,gf(x),1\rangle\in S. So f(gx)=g(f(x)).
  2. Take x in a class C disjoint from N, and y'\in C\setminus\{x,f(x)\}. Let g be the transposition of f(x) and y'. It fixes N and x, so f(x)=f(gx)=g(f(x))=y', a contradiction.

So the closed pure sentence

P:=\forall x\,\exists y\,(x\ne y\land\Diamond x=y)\land\neg\exists F\,\bigl(\operatorname{Functional}(F)\land\forall x\,y\,(Fxy\to x\ne y\land\Diamond x=y)\bigr)

is true at the evaluation point: the first conjunct by the classes being infinite, the second by the argument above. This is the consistency premise of maximalism-incompatible-with-modalized-relational-choice.

Other verdicts

  • No Pure Contingency holds: the model has one object (general argument).
  • BF holds at every type: the arrows are surjective (the group’s argument barcan-surjective).
  • Relational Choice and Transversal fail: these arguments use only the symmetry group, which is that of symmetric-infinite-classes.
  • Intensional Choice fails at type e, by a different witness from symmetric-infinite-classes, whose arrows can merge classes.
    • Take F:=\lambda y\, .\,y\ne n_0\land y\mathrel E n_0.
    • Use the arrow that collapses N\cap[n_0] onto n_0 within its class.
    • A transposition inside [n_0] that avoids the image of the pinning set then moves the supposed unique instance.
  • Atomlessness holds. Split a possible proposition by whether two fresh points of one class are sent into one class.
  • The Axiom of Infinity at type e holds, by the group’s argument.

Weakly Inextensible Comprehension fails

Added later on 16 October, at Cian Dorr’s request to see whether the arguments for Boolean Completeness and Inextensible Comprehension in symmetric-all-surjections (Base 1) carry over. They do not.

  • Boolean Completeness. Base 1 gets it from Dorr’s hull conditions, and (B1) fails here.
  • Inextensible Comprehension. Base 1’s argument needs a transversal lemma: an arrow g fixing S pointwise has, for the points of a finite T, preimages that a symmetry can move them to. When g splits one class over two target classes, the preimages of points in both lie in one class, and no automorphism of E does the job.

In fact both principles fail, through Weakly Inextensible Comprehension at type e\to t.

Let X:=\lambda x\, .\,\neg\, x\mathrel E n_0. It is in the domain, since E is. Suppose Y is weakly inextensible, coextensive with X, and pinned down by a finite N.

  1. Y\le Z^m for each m\in[n_0]. Put Z^m:=\lambda x\, .\,\neg\, x\mathrel E n_0\land\neg\, x\mathrel E m. Its extension at an arrow k is \mathbb N\setminus([kn_0]\cup[km]). If y is an instance of Y, so y\notin[n_0], then ky\notin[kn_0]\cup[km] at every arrow k, because k reflects E. So Z^m holds necessarily of every instance of Y, and weak inextensibility gives Y\le Z^m.
  2. An arrow sending part of [n_0] into a fresh class. Take a class D\ne[n_0] disjoint from N, and m\in[n_0]\setminus N. Let k be:
    • on [n_0], a map onto [n_0]\cup D that fixes N\cap[n_0] and sends m into D;
    • the identity on the other classes that meet N;
    • on the remaining classes, which are infinitely many, a map onto the remaining classes other than D, class by class.
    Then k is a surjection, the preimage of each class lies in a single class, and k agrees with the identity on N.
  3. The contradiction. By pinning and step 1, Y_1=Y_k\subseteq Z^m_k=\mathbb N\setminus([n_0]\cup D). But Y_1=\mathbb N\setminus[n_0]\supseteq D.

So Weakly Inextensible Comprehension fails. So do Inextensible Comprehension, which implies it, and Boolean Completeness, since Boolean Completeness implies it too.

The recorded proof of that last implication takes the least upper bound of the haecceities of the instances of X. That family is not countable in this model, so the argument says nothing about Countable Boolean Completeness.

Left open

  • Countable Boolean Completeness. As in symmetric-old-new-individuals, a countable property’s instances are invariant under the symmetries fixing a finite set. The step from invariance to uniform pinning has to deal with arrows that split classes.
  • The rigidity family: Tame Rigidity, Rigid Power, Rigid Rigidity and their weak forms. Their recorded arguments assume all surjections or section arrows. Section arrows fail here: when an arrow splits one class across two target classes, a section of points in both would have to merge classes.
