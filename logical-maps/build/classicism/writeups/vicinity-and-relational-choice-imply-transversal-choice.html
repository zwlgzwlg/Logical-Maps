Vicinity and Relational Choice imply Transversal Choice

The theorem is due to GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI), prompted by Daniel Kodsi, in a research note of 26 October 2026 in Daniel Kodsi’s pull request #18. Astra’s proof interprets C5 inside C plus Vicinity and proves the result for C5. The direct proof below was written by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), prompted by Cian Dorr, 26–28 October 2026. It does not use the interpretation. Its collection lemma is Christopher Sun’s argument for the 5-necessity form of Vicinity.

Claim. For every type \sigma, including e, C together with Vicinity and Relational Choice at types (\sigma t,\sigma) proves Transversal Choice at \sigma. Every premise is unboxed, and the whole argument takes place at the evaluation point.

The idea. The Zermelo-style argument grows a partial transversal by adding chosen points, and partial transversals are compared by \le, necessary inclusion. That order is antisymmetric, so coextensive but distinct partial transversals cause no trouble. In C, what fails is the step to suprema: the union of a chain need not be its least upper bound under \le. Vicinity supplies a truth s that settles every identity statement and lets universal quantifiers commute with “s\le\cdots”. That is enough for suprema of chains whose membership is settled by s. The Bourbaki–Witt lemma for admissible chains (Background, Relations, orders and fixed points) needs suprema only for such chains, provided the properties its proof forms are settled too, and they are.

What Vicinity provides

Let v witness Vicinity, so that v is true and \forall q\, .\,q\to v\le\Diamond q. Put

A:=\forall r\, .\,r\to v\le\Diamond r,\qquad s:=v\land A .

Both conjuncts are true, so s is true. Call a proposition p settled if s\le p or s\le\neg p, and a property Y^{\tau t} settled if each of its instances is:

\operatorname{Settled}(p):=s\le p\lor s\le\neg p,\qquad \operatorname{Settled}(Y):=\forall b^\tau\, .\,\operatorname{Settled}(Yb).

Because s is true, a settled proposition is true if and only if s\le p. Call this fact (∗).

Lemma 1 (s settles identities). For any a,b of the same type, \operatorname{Settled}(a=b).

Proof. If a=b is true, it is \top by NI. If it is false, A applied to the truth a\ne b gives v\le\Diamond(a\ne b), and by NI \Diamond(a\ne b)\le(a\ne b). So s\le v\le(a\ne b). \square

Lemma 2 (collection; Sun). For F^{\tau t}: if s\le Fx for every x^\tau, then s\le\forall x\, .\,Fx.

Proof. Let T:=\forall x\, .\,s\le Fx, which is true. Since v\land T is true and A is true, E:=(v\le\Diamond(v\land T)) is true. Being an identity, E is \top. It therefore suffices to show \vdash E\to(s\to\forall x\, .\,Fx), since necessitation then gives s\le\forall x\, .\,Fx.

Assume E, s and \neg Fx. Since s includes A, applying A to s\land\neg Fx gives G:=(v\le\Diamond(s\land\neg Fx)). This is an identity, so \Box G. From v and E we get \Diamond(v\land T), hence \Diamond(v\land T\land G). At such a world, v and G give \Diamond(s\land\neg Fx), while T gives \Box(s\to Fx) by instantiation. These are inconsistent. So \vdash E\land s\land\neg Fx\to\bot, and generalizing on x gives \vdash E\land s\land\exists x\, .\,\neg Fx\to\bot. \square

Lemma 3 (settledness spreads). Let Y_1,\dots,Y_n be variables of property types, and let \varphi be a formula built by truth-functional connectives and quantifiers from atoms of two kinds:

  • identities a=b, where a and b are any terms;
  • atoms Y_ib, where b is any term.

Quantifiers binding a Y_i must be restricted to settled properties, in the form \forall Y_i\, .\,\operatorname{Settled}(Y_i)\to\psi or \exists Y_i\, .\,\operatorname{Settled}(Y_i)\land\psi. Quantifiers binding other variables are unrestricted. Then it is a theorem of C, universally closed over the free variables, that if v witnesses Vicinity, then

\operatorname{Settled}(Y_1)\land\cdots\land\operatorname{Settled}(Y_n)\to\operatorname{Settled}(\varphi).

Proof. By induction on \varphi, for all lists Y_1,\dots,Y_n at once. Identities are settled by Lemma 1, and atoms Y_ib by the hypothesis on Y_i. Connectives are immediate.

Take \forall x\, .\,\varphi(x), where x is not among the Y_i. Every instance \varphi(a) is settled, by induction. If every instance is true, then by (∗) s\le\varphi(a) for every a, so s\le\forall x\, .\,\varphi(x) by Lemma 2. If some instance \varphi(a) is false, then by (∗) s\le\neg\varphi(a)\le\neg\forall x\, .\,\varphi(x). The existential quantifier is dual.

Take \forall Y\, .\,\operatorname{Settled}(Y)\to\psi. The formula \operatorname{Settled}(Y) is built from identities (s\le Yb is the identity s\land Yb=s), so it is settled. If it is false, then s\le\neg\operatorname{Settled}(Y), and the instance \operatorname{Settled}(Y)\to\psi is settled. If it is true, the induction hypothesis for \psi, with Y added to the list, shows that \psi is settled, and so is the instance. So every instance is settled, and the previous case applies. The restricted existential quantifier is dual. \square

Lemmas 1–3 are all that Vicinity contributes.

The construction

Fix \sigma and an equivalence relation R^{\sigma\sigma t}, which need only be an equivalence relation actually. The variables B,D,\dots range over properties of type \sigma t. Put

(Qx)y:=\forall z\, .\,(Rx)z\leftrightarrow(Ry)z,\qquad \overline B:=\lambda x\, .\,\exists y\, .\,(Qx)y\land By,

\operatorname{PT}(B):=\forall x\,y\, .\,Bx\land By\land(Qx)y\to x=y,\qquad \operatorname{Full}(B):=\forall x\, .\,\overline Bx .

Q is an equivalence relation by pure logic, hence necessarily, and it is actually coextensive with R, since R is actually an equivalence relation. A property B with \operatorname{PT}(B)\land\operatorname{Full}(B) is therefore a transversal of R. We use Q rather than R so that its symmetry and transitivity can be used under \Box.

Candidates. B is a candidate if B\le\lambda x\, .\,s and \Box\operatorname{PT}(B). That is, every instance of B entails s, and B is necessarily a partial transversal of Q.

The step. The relation (UB)a:=\operatorname{Full}(B)\lor\neg\overline Ba is serial: if B is not full, some a lies outside \overline B. Relational Choice gives a functional K^{(\sigma t)\sigma t} with K\subseteq U. For each B put

D_B:=\lambda x\, .\,s\land(KB)x\land\bigl(\forall y\, .\,(KB)y\land(Qx)y\to y=x\bigr) \land\neg\overline Bx,\qquad fB:=\lambda x\, .\,Bx\lor D_Bx .

The uniqueness clause in D_B is needed because K is functional only actually, while candidates must be partial transversals necessarily. Here f is a term of type (\sigma t)\sigma t with s and K as parameters. So statements such as D=fB and fB\le D are identity statements, whatever K is.

Applying the Bourbaki–Witt lemma

Apply the Bourbaki–Witt lemma for admissible chains at type \sigma t, with:

  • \mathcal A:=\operatorname{Settled}, so the admissible properties are the settled ones;
  • X:=\lambda B\, .\,B is a candidate;
  • (RB)D:=B\le D;
  • (FB)D:=(D=fB).

Here \le is antisymmetric (Intensionality) and F is functional, so this is the special case of partial orders: \approx is identity on the candidates. If the hypotheses hold, part (c) of the lemma gives a candidate m with fm=m.

That m is a transversal of R. Suppose \operatorname{Full}(m) were false. Let a be the actual K-value of m, so (Km)a. Since K\subseteq U, \neg\overline ma. Each conjunct of D_ma is then true: s is true, and the uniqueness clause holds because K is functional. So (fm)a is true. But ma is false, since m is included in \overline m by the reflexivity of Q. So fm\ne m, a contradiction. Hence \operatorname{Full}(m). Since m is a candidate, \operatorname{PT}(m) is true. So m has exactly one instance in each Q-cell. As Q is actually coextensive with R, m is a transversal of R, which proves Transversal Choice at \sigma.

It remains to check the hypotheses of the lemma.

Hypothesis 1: R preorders the candidates

\le is reflexive and transitive, and it is antisymmetric as well.

Hypothesis 2: every settled chain has a supremum

Let Z be a settled property of properties, with Z\subseteq X and \operatorname{Total}(R,Z). Its members are the B with ZB true, or equivalently, by (∗), the B with s\le ZB. Put

J:=\lambda x\, .\,s\land\exists B\, .\,ZB\land Bx .

J bounds the members. If ZB is true, then s\le ZB, and this identity is \top. Since B\le\lambda x\, .\,s, pointwise Bx\le s\land ZB\land Bx\le Jx. So B\le J.

J is a candidate. J\le\lambda x\, .\,s is clear. For \Box\operatorname{PT}(J), it suffices to show s\le\operatorname{PT}(J): since Jx entails s, \neg s\to\operatorname{PT}(J) is a theorem. By Lemma 2, applied to each of the four quantifiers, it suffices to show, for all x,y,B,B',

s\le\bigl(ZB\land ZB'\land Bx\land B'y\land(Qx)y\to x=y\bigr).

If B or B' is not a member, this holds because s\le\neg ZB or s\le\neg ZB', as Z is settled. If both are members, they are comparable, say B\le B'. Then Bx entails B'x, and since B' is a candidate, s\le\operatorname{PT}(B')\le(B'x\land B'y\land(Qx)y\to x=y).

J is the least candidate bounding the members. Let D be a candidate with B\le D for every member B. For each B,

s\le\bigl(ZB\to\forall x\, .\,Bx\to Dx\bigr):

for a member because B\le D, and for a non-member because s\le\neg ZB. Lemma 2 gives s\le\forall B\, .\,ZB\to\forall x\, .\,Bx\to Dx, so s\le\forall x\, .\,Jx\to Dx. Where s is false, J has no instances, so J\le D.

So J is a supremum of Z in X. The settledness of Z was used twice, both times to apply Lemma 2. Without it, the union of a chain bounds it only at the evaluation point.

Hypothesis 3: F is progressive

Let B be a candidate. Its only F-value is fB, and B\le fB. To see that fB is a candidate:

  • fB\le\lambda x\, .\,s, since B and D_B both are.
  • \operatorname{PT}(B)\to\operatorname{PT}(fB) is a theorem, so \Box\operatorname{PT}(fB) follows from \Box\operatorname{PT}(B).

Here is the theorem. Two Q-related instances of D_B are identical, by the uniqueness clause of D_B. An instance of B cannot be Q-related to an instance of D_B, since instances of D_B lie outside \overline B and Q is symmetric. Two Q-related instances of B are identical by \operatorname{PT}(B).

Since F is functional and \le antisymmetric, invariance is automatic.

The new hypothesis: the properties the proof forms are settled

The variant requires X, the tower M, the property of being a pivot, and each M_{c,e} to be coextensive with admissible properties. In fact each is itself settled, by Lemma 3.

Write out the definitions with the present X, R and F. Then every atom is one of three kinds:

  • an identity, such as B\le D, D=fB, or one of the two identities that make up “B is a candidate”;
  • an atom YB, where Y is bound by the quantifier \forall Y\, .\,\mathcal AY\land\operatorname{Closed}(Y)\to\cdots of the tower;
  • an atom ZB, where Z is bound in the clause of closed for suprema of admissible chains.

Both kinds of bound variable are restricted to settled properties, since \mathcal A=\operatorname{Settled}. So Lemma 3, with n=0, shows that each of these properties is settled at every argument. \blacksquare

Remarks

Where the premises are used. Vicinity is used only through Lemmas 1–3. Relational Choice is used only to show that a candidate fixed by f is full. Nothing is boxed.

Consequences. ND at type t implies Vicinity, so ND at type t and Relational Choice give Transversal Choice; in particular C5 and Relational Choice do, and there s=\top, Lemma 1 being ND and Lemma 2 BF. Since Transversal Choice is equivalent to Transversal together with Relational Choice, any model separating Relational Choice from Transversal Choice must falsify Vicinity.

Astra’s route. Astra’s proof interprets C5 inside C plus Vicinity: propositions are restricted to those below s, and necessity becomes identity with s. It then proves C5 and Relational Choice imply Transversal Choice by a fixed-point argument over rigid families, and transfers Relational Choice into the interpretation and the transversal back out. Lemmas 1–3 are the core of that interpretation, and the fixed-point argument has to deal with rigidity in the same places as Hypothesis 2 and the new hypothesis above. Proving the result directly avoids the soundness proof for the interpretation and the transfer steps.
