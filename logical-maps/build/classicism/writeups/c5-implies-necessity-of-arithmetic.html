C5 ⇒ Necessity of Arithmetic

Theorem 4.4 of Andrew Bacon, Could the truths of mathematics have been different? (draft of 9 October 2025, p. 19; proved as Theorem C.1 in Appendix C.1, pp. 10–11 of the separately paginated appendix, through Proposition C.2 and Lemma C.1), transposed to the map’s formulation of the Necessity of Arithmetic, which follows Goodsell (Background). Transposition by Claude Fable 5.1, prompted by Zachary Goodsell (Anthropic), 19 October 2026. Lean-verified the same day: the steps below are derived shallow theorems (lean/Classicism/Results/SentenceSchemas/Arithmetic.lean), and the induction on arithmetical sentences composes their derivations (ArithmeticMeta.lean, c5_entails_necessityOfArithmetic; certificate Classicism.Map.c5_implies_necessity_of_arithmetic).

Bacon’s logic \mathrm H\Box.5 is his background logic with T, 4 and B necessitated. C5, which is C with □ND at every type, contains it: □ND gives □5, hence □B (recorded), BF at every type (necessary-nd-implies-bf) and ND at every type. His theorem concerns first-order arithmetic over natural-number structures of individuals, and its proof passes through the canonical structure of numerical quantifiers, which he shows rigid in S5 (Proposition C.2) before an induction on sentences (Lemma C.1). The map’s arithmetical sentences are already about the canonical structure, the finite cardinalities \mathbb N_\sigma of properties of a type \sigma, with \operatorname{Sum}_\sigma and \operatorname{Prod}_\sigma defined by recursion, so the argument below runs his Proposition C.2 and Lemma C.1 directly on it. It proves more than the recorded principle: in C5 every arithmetical sentence is necessary or impossible outright, with no antecedent I_\sigma.

Fix \sigma and drop the subscripts; write n^+ for \operatorname{Suc}n and \nu for (\sigma t)t. A term is a variable of type \nu, \mathbf 0, or the successor of a term; when the variables of a term take numbers as values so does the term, since \mathbb Nn\to\mathbb Nn^+ is a theorem of C. Besides theorems of C (T, 4, K, NI, CBF), the argument uses ND at type \nu, the dual of B (\Diamond\Box p\to p) and BF at type \nu, all at the evaluation point, and all supplied by C5.

Lemma 1 (numbers are necessarily numbers; C). \forall n\,.\,\mathbb Nn\to\Box\mathbb Nn. By the definition of \mathbb N with the property \lambda n\,.\,\Box\mathbb Nn: \Box\mathbb N\mathbf 0 since \mathbb N\mathbf 0 is a theorem, and \Box\mathbb Nn\to\Box\mathbb Nn^+ by K from the necessitated theorem \mathbb Nn\to\mathbb Nn^+. (Lemma 3 of the write-up for countable-boolean-completeness-implies-necessity-of-arithmetic.)

Lemma 2 (non-numbers are necessarily non-numbers; C5). \forall n\,.\,\neg\mathbb Nn\to\Box\neg\mathbb Nn. Contrapose: if \Diamond\mathbb Nn, then \Diamond\Box\mathbb Nn by the necessitation of Lemma 1 and K, so \mathbb Nn by the dual of B.

Lemma 3 (sums and products are necessary when true; C). For numbers m,n,o: \operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o)\to\Box\operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o) and \operatorname{Prod}(m,n,o)\to\Box\operatorname{Prod}(m,n,o). For \operatorname{Sum}, instantiate its definition at R:=\lambda i\,j\,.\,\Box\operatorname{Sum}(m,i,j). R\mathbf 0m holds, since \operatorname{Sum}(m,\mathbf 0,m) is a theorem of C (the consequent of its definition is its own antecedent) and so necessary. R is closed under the recursion on numbers: for numbers i,j, \operatorname{Sum}(m,i,j)\to\operatorname{Sum}(m,i^+,j^+) is a theorem of C, since any R' with R'\mathbf 0m that is closed under the recursion has R'ij and hence R'i^+j^+; necessitating it and using Lemma 1 for i and j, \Box\operatorname{Sum}(m,i,j)\to\Box\operatorname{Sum}(m,i^+,j^+). So \operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o) gives Rno. For \operatorname{Prod}, instantiate at R:=\lambda i\,j\,.\,\Box\operatorname{Prod}(m,i,j): R\mathbf 0\mathbf 0 since \operatorname{Prod}(m,\mathbf 0,\mathbf 0) is a theorem; and for numbers i,j,k, \operatorname{Prod}(m,i,j)\to\operatorname{Sum}(m,j,k)\to\operatorname{Prod}(m,i^+,k) is a theorem, so with Lemma 1 and the case of \operatorname{Sum}, \Box\operatorname{Prod}(m,i,j)\to\operatorname{Sum}(m,j,k)\to\Box\operatorname{Prod}(m,i^+,k), which is the recursion clause for R.

Lemma 4 (sums and products are impossible when false; C5). For numbers m,n,o: \neg\operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o)\to\Box\neg\operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o), and likewise for \operatorname{Prod}. Contrapose: \Diamond\operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o) gives \Diamond\Box\operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o) by the necessitation of Lemma 3, whose hypotheses \mathbb Nm, \mathbb Nn, \mathbb No are necessary by Lemma 1, and then \operatorname{Sum}(m,n,o) by the dual of B.

Lemma 5 (identities). For any m,n of type \nu: m=n\to\Box\,m=n (NI) and m\ne n\to\Box\,m\ne n (ND at \nu).

Lemma 6 (arithmetical formulas are non-contingent on numbers; C5). For every arithmetical formula A with free variables \bar v: \forall\bar v\in\mathbb N\,.\,(A\to\Box A)\land(\neg A\to\Box\neg A). By induction on A, for all values of \bar v in \mathbb N at once. Atomic formulas: \mathbb N\mathbf t by Lemmas 1 and 2; \operatorname{Sum}(\mathbf m,\mathbf n,\mathbf o) and \operatorname{Prod}(\mathbf m,\mathbf n,\mathbf o) by Lemmas 3 and 4, the values of the terms being numbers; and \mathbf m=\mathbf n by Lemma 5. Negation is immediate; for A\land B, \Box A\land\Box B gives \Box(A\land B), and \Box\neg A or \Box\neg B gives \Box\neg(A\land B), by K; disjunction likewise. For \forall v\in\mathbb N\,.\,A: if it holds, then for every v, either \mathbb Nv and \Box A by the hypothesis, or \neg\mathbb Nv and \Box\neg\mathbb Nv by Lemma 2; either way \Box(\mathbb Nv\to A), so \forall v\,.\,\Box(\mathbb Nv\to A), and BF at \nu gives \Box\forall v\,.\,\mathbb Nv\to A. If it fails, some number v has \neg A, so \Box\neg A by the hypothesis and \Box\mathbb Nv by Lemma 1, whence \Box\exists v\,.\,\mathbb Nv\land\neg A by K. The existential quantifier is the dual.

Theorem. For every arithmetical sentence A at \sigma, \Box A\lor\Box\neg A in C5, and so \Box(I_\sigma\to A)\lor\Box(I_\sigma\to\neg A). The first is Lemma 6 for a sentence; the second follows by K. \blacksquare

Remarks

  • Beyond C the argument uses only the dual of B (Lemmas 2 and 4), ND at \nu (Lemma 5) and BF at \nu (the quantifier step), all at the evaluation point. Bacon states the theorem for \mathrm H\Box.5, without Intensionality; it is recorded with the preset C5, the map’s name for that logic over C.
  • Bacon’s Theorem 4.3 shows that the result fails in second-order modal logic with S5, where the numerical quantifiers need not be rigid; the higher-order comprehension behind Lemma 1, the property \lambda n\,.\,\Box\mathbb Nn of cardinalities, is what makes the difference.
